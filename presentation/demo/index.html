Diabetes Mellitus

How insulin, blood glucose, and health are connected

Example Student

BIOL 110 · The Community College of Baltimore County

Why does blood glucose matter?

  • Cells use glucose as an important fuel
  • Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals
  • The body keeps it within a healthy range through coordinated hormone signals

Diabetes mellitus is a group of conditions in which blood glucose stays too high because insulin is absent, insufficient, or not working effectively.

Start with the body’s everyday task of keeping glucose available to cells without allowing too much to remain in the blood. Clarify that this talk is about diabetes mellitus, not diabetes insipidus, which is a different condition.

Normal glucose regulation

After a meal

  • Blood glucose increases
  • Pancreatic beta cells release insulin
  • Body tissues take up and use or store more glucose
  • The liver reduces glucose release

Between meals

  • Blood glucose begins to fall
  • The pancreas releases glucagon
  • The liver releases stored glucose
  • Blood glucose is supported between meals

Insulin and glucagon have complementary roles. Keep the model simple: insulin supports glucose uptake and storage, while glucagon signals the liver to make glucose available.

What changes in diabetes?

  • Glucose remains in the bloodstream instead of being regulated normally
  • The cause involves insulin production, insulin action, or both
  • Persistent hyperglycemia can affect many organs over time

Diabetes mellitus is not one single disease. Type 1, type 2, and gestational diabetes have different causes and care needs.

Emphasize that a shared feature, high blood glucose, does not mean every type has the same cause or treatment.

The major types

Type Main biological change Key point
Type 1 Immune system destroys pancreatic beta cells Insulin is required for survival
Type 2 Insulin resistance plus declining beta-cell function The most common type; several factors contribute
Gestational High blood glucose first recognized during pregnancy Requires care during pregnancy and follow-up afterward

These are broad patterns, not a way to diagnose someone from appearance or behavior. Other less common forms also exist.

Type 1 diabetes

  • An autoimmune process damages the pancreatic beta cells that make insulin
  • The body produces little or no insulin
  • It can begin at any age and is not caused by eating sugar or by a person’s choices
  • Insulin therapy is essential

Avoid describing type 1 as only a childhood condition. Explain that autoimmune destruction of beta cells leads to severe insulin deficiency.

Type 2 diabetes

  • Body tissues become less responsive to insulin: insulin resistance
  • The pancreas may initially compensate by making more insulin
  • Over time, beta cells may not meet the body’s needs
  • Risk reflects interacting factors, including genetics, age, health history, and environment

Type 2 is not simply a consequence of one food, body size, or behavior. Many people have risk factors they cannot control, and it can occur at any age.

Gestational diabetes

  • Develops during pregnancy when the body cannot make enough insulin to meet increased needs
  • Often has no obvious symptoms, so screening matters
  • Care helps manage risks to the pregnant person and baby
  • Blood glucose should be rechecked after birth; future type 2 risk is higher

Present gestational diabetes without blame. It commonly improves after pregnancy, but follow-up testing is important because risk remains elevated.

Possible signs and symptoms

  • Increased thirst and urination
  • Increased hunger or unexplained weight loss
  • Fatigue or blurry vision
  • Slow-healing sores or recurrent infections

Some people, especially with type 2 diabetes, have no noticeable symptoms for a long time.

Symptoms are not enough to diagnose diabetes. Sudden severe symptoms, vomiting, confusion, or difficulty breathing need urgent medical attention.

How is diabetes diagnosed?

Test Diabetes-range result
A1C 6.5% or higher
Fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL or higher after at least 8 hours without calories
2-hour oral glucose tolerance test 200 mg/dL or higher after a 75 g glucose drink
Random plasma glucose 200 mg/dL or higher with classic symptoms or hyperglycemic crisis

These are standard diagnostic thresholds for nonpregnant people. Pregnancy uses distinct screening and diagnostic approaches. Diagnosis and test selection belong with a qualified health professional.

For someone without unequivocal hyperglycemia, an abnormal result is generally confirmed with repeat testing.

What does A1C tell us?

  • A1C estimates average blood glucose over roughly the past 2–3 months
  • It does not show day-to-day highs and lows
  • A1C can be misleading when red blood cell lifespan or hemoglobin is affected
  • Other tests may be preferred in some situations

Examples that can affect A1C interpretation include some anemias, hemoglobin variants, recent blood loss or transfusion, and pregnancy. The clinician interprets the result in context.

Interpreting a result

A fictional adult has a fasting plasma glucose of 130 mg/dL. A repeat test on another day is 128 mg/dL.

  • Both results are above the fasting diagnostic threshold of 126 mg/dL
  • Repeating an abnormal result helps confirm it when there are no clear symptoms
  • A clinician interprets the results alongside health history and other evidence

This is an educational example, not a self-diagnosis exercise. Ask the class which threshold applies and why confirmation matters.

Why long-term glucose levels matter

Over time, diabetes can damage blood vessels and nerves, increasing the risk of:

  • Eye disease and vision loss
  • Kidney disease
  • Nerve damage, including in the feet
  • Heart disease and stroke

Risk is not inevitable. Blood glucose management and regular preventive care can reduce the likelihood or severity of complications.

Management is individualized

  • Type 1 diabetes requires insulin
  • Type 2 care may include nutrition and activity changes, medicines, insulin, or combinations
  • Monitoring, sleep, stress, and support can all matter
  • Regular checks help protect the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels

There is no single plan that is right for everyone. Care is based on the person’s health, goals, and circumstances.

Avoid presenting a specific diet, medication, or glucose target as universal advice. People should make care decisions with their health-care team.

Can type 2 diabetes be prevented?

  • Healthy eating patterns and regular physical activity can lower risk for some people
  • Structured prevention programs can help people at high risk
  • Risk can be reduced, but prevention is not guaranteed
  • There is currently no established way to prevent type 1 diabetes

Use nonjudgmental language. Prevention programs change probabilities; they do not assign fault or guarantee an outcome.

Take-home points

  • Insulin helps regulate blood glucose; diabetes disrupts this regulation
  • Type 1, type 2, and gestational diabetes have different causes
  • Laboratory testing, not symptoms or appearance alone, is used for diagnosis
  • Early care and ongoing management reduce the risk of complications

Close by returning to the central idea: diabetes is a group of conditions involving glucose regulation, and understanding the type matters for care.
